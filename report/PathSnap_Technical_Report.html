1. Overview

PathSnap matches noisy GPS traces to a directed road network. The pipeline is: (1) clean the track and project it to metres; (2) generate up to 12 candidate road arcs within 50 m of every GPS point; (3) compute 21 geometric and kinematic features per (point, candidate); (4) score candidates with a classifier (five models compared, the best tuned with Bayesian optimisation); (5) decode a connected route with an HMM/Viterbi decoder on the road graph; (6) serve the pipeline through a FastAPI backend (Laptop 1) and a Streamlit UI (Laptop 2). Final model: LightGBM (gradient boosting); decoder: beta = 20.0, emission weight = 2.0.

1. How did you preprocess the data?

  • Validation (notebook 01, both partitions): 0 missing values, 0 invalid coordinates, 0 invalid arc/route ids, 0 duplicate GPS rows, 0 non-increasing timestamps. The same rules run again inside the pipeline (src/data_io.py::clean_track: finite, plausible lon/lat, de-duplicate, sort and keep strictly increasing time), so new trips go through identical preprocessing.
  • Projection: lon/lat are angles, and the trips are spread over many countries (Europe, Russia, Ukraine, the Americas). Each trip is therefore projected onto its own local equirectangular plane around the trip centroid. At city scale the error is far below GPS noise, and all distances are in metres.
  • Candidate generation: arcs are sampled every 10 m into a KD-tree. Every GPS point gets all arcs within 50 m (max 12, nearest first). If none exists, the nearest arcs are used and flagged (is_fallback). Result: 7.5 (train) / 7.7 (student-test) candidates per point; candidate miss rate 0.0728 (train) and 0.0021 (student-test).
  • Labels: .route is an ordered arc list without timestamps. Each GPS point is assigned a route position by a monotonic dynamic-programming alignment (positions only move forward in time; the sum of point-to-arc distances, capped at 100 m, is minimised). A candidate is positive if it equals the aligned arc (0.124 / 0.130 positives).
  • Unreliable labels: observations whose aligned reference arc is more than 50 m away get label_ok = 0 (0.111 of train observations, 0.0014 of student-test). They are excluded from training but kept in evaluation.
  • Subsampling: 1 Hz points are highly redundant, so every 2nd point of the training trips is used (104,229 observations, 776,903 candidate rows). Student-test keeps every point (19,160 observations).

2. What patterns did you observe? Which features did you extract and why?

  • Sampling is nominally 1 Hz (mean share of 1-s steps per trip in the table), but there are 617 gaps longer than 10 s, up to 153064 s. Trips 00000090 and 00000078 (train) contain gaps of about 42 h and 15 h, i.e. several recordings joined in one file. The HMM widens its search when dt > 10 s and restarts the chain when no transition is feasible.
  • Road networks differ in size by two orders of magnitude (649 - 307,392 arcs per trip, figure 2.3), and most networks contain several weakly connected components and some isolated nodes. Transitions must therefore use directed shortest paths, not straight lines.
  • The reference routes have 14 disconnected consecutive arc pairs (gaps of 10-596 m, notebook 01). They are reference data, not verified ground truth.
  • Points without a usable label: in train trip 00000054 (19,313 points, the longest trip) the vehicle is parked off the road network for most of the recording. 86% of its points are more than 50 m from any road, and 92% of those move below 2 m/s: a dense stationary cloud in a parking area next to where the reference route ends (figure 2.4). In train trip 00000033 the GPS is on the road network (only 1% of points are more than 50 m from a road), but about 59% of the points are more than 50 m from the reference route, so the route covers only part of the driven path. These two trips explain the low plain Top-1 of CV fold 0, and they are why Top-1 is reported both on all observations and on reliably-labelled ones (label_ok).

Features (all computable without the route):

  • Distance features: dist_m, dist_rank, dist_minus_min, dist_ratio, n_cands. These encode the emission idea "the closer arc is more likely", both in absolute terms and relative to the other candidates of the same point.
  • Temporal context: dist_prev_m, dist_next_m, dist_window_mean, the distance of the same arc to the neighbouring GPS points. A true arc stays close over several seconds, while a crossing street is close only briefly.
  • Direction: cos_heading, abs_heading_diff, cos_heading_long, speed_mps, heading_valid. The GPS travel direction (from neighbouring points, 1 s and 5 s windows) compared with the arc bearing. This is the only way to separate the two directed arcs of a two-way street, and heading is ignored below 1.5 m/s, when it is noise.
  • Geometry/topology: arc_len, proj_frac, overhang_m, out_deg_end, in_deg_start, has_twin, plus quality flags dt_prev, is_fallback.

The importance plot (figure 2.6) confirms that the context-distance and heading features carry most of the signal, beyond raw distance.

3. Model Experimentation and Comparison

Protocol. Five models from five algorithm families were trained on the same feature table with seed 42. Each model gets 5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation on the 80 training trips (groups = trip_id, so no trip appears in both training and validation), then a refit on all training rows and one evaluation on the 10 student-test trips. Classification metrics use threshold 0.5. Top-1 takes the highest-scoring candidate per GPS observation and compares it with the aligned reference arc. Route continuity is the share of consecutive Top-1 arcs that are identical or directly connected (end node = start node). Inference time is measured over the whole student-test candidate table.

Primary selection metric: student-test observation-level Top-1 accuracy. It is exactly the map-matching objective (one road segment per GPS point), whereas macro F1 is threshold-dependent and counts every candidate. Log loss is the second criterion because the HMM uses the probabilities as emission scores.

3.2 Compulsory Questions for Every Model

3.2.1 Logistic Regression (Linear / probabilistic)

A1. Why did you consider this algorithm suitable for the PathSnap problem?

Each candidate is scored from a handful of geometric features (perpendicular distance, heading agreement, distance to neighbouring GPS points). These relate fairly monotonically to the probability that a candidate is correct, so a linear model on standardised features is a natural, fully interpretable baseline. It also gives calibrated probabilities, which the HMM needs as emission scores.

A2. Major strengths and limitations for this task

Strengths: trains in seconds, smallest inference cost of all five models, stable across folds, and the coefficients can be inspected. Limitations: it can only build a linear decision boundary in feature space. Interactions such as "heading only matters when the vehicle is moving" (speed x heading) or the ranking structure within one observation cannot be represented. This shows in its lower Top-1 compared with the tree ensembles.

B5. Important hyperparameters and initial values

C = 1.0 (inverse L2 strength), penalty = L2, solver = lbfgs, max_iter = 2000, StandardScaler on all 21 features, no class weighting (keeps probabilities calibrated).

B6. Loss function / optimisation objective

Binary cross-entropy (log loss) with an L2 penalty, minimised by L-BFGS.

C7. Performance on the student-testing dataset

On the 10 student-test trips (19,160 GPS observations, 147,061 candidate rows): candidate-level accuracy 0.9615, macro precision 0.9111, macro recall 0.9210, positive-class F1 0.8541. Confusion matrix (rows = true 0/1, cols = predicted 0/1): [[124,840, 3,102], [2,558, 16,561]]. Route continuity of its classifier-only Top-1 path is 0.9324. Inference takes 0.0013 ms per GPS observation; the full refit took 2.6 s.

C8. Observation-level Top-1 accuracy, macro F1 and log loss (student-test)

Top-1 = 0.8727 (on reliably-labelled observations: 0.8739), macro F1 = 0.9159, log loss = 0.1029. CV (5-fold GroupKFold over the 80 training trips): macro F1 0.8952 ± 0.0439 (folds 0.8174-0.9222), Top-1 on labelled observations 0.8801.

C9. Overfitting or underfitting? Evidence

Mean training-fold macro F1 is 0.9204 against validation 0.8952 (gap 0.0252), and student-test macro F1 is 0.9159. The small gap means little overfitting. The lower Top-1 compared with the tree ensembles points to some underfitting from the model's limited capacity.

3.2.2 K-Nearest Neighbours (distance-weighted) (Instance-based)

A1. Why did you consider this algorithm suitable for the PathSnap problem?

Map matching is a similarity problem: a candidate whose feature vector (distance, heading match, context distances) resembles past correct candidates is probably correct. KNN makes no parametric assumption and lets us test whether local similarity in the scaled feature space is enough.

A2. Major strengths and limitations for this task

Strengths: no training phase, non-linear decision boundary, easy to explain. Limitations: with 21 dimensions the distances become less informative (curse of dimensionality). Prediction cost grows with the training set, so we had to subsample 200,000 training rows, and it is by far the slowest model at inference. Distance weighting gives every training point weight 1 on itself, so its training F1 is 1.0 by construction (not real evidence of overfitting).

B5. Important hyperparameters and initial values

n_neighbors = 50, weights = distance, Euclidean (Minkowski p=2) distance, StandardScaler, random subsample of 200,000 training rows (MAX_TRAIN_ROWS_KNN).

B6. Loss function / optimisation objective

None (lazy learner): the probability is the distance-weighted share of positive neighbours. Evaluated with log loss.

C7. Performance on the student-testing dataset

On the 10 student-test trips (19,160 GPS observations, 147,061 candidate rows): candidate-level accuracy 0.9633, macro precision 0.9236, macro recall 0.9122, positive-class F1 0.8566. Confusion matrix (rows = true 0/1, cols = predicted 0/1): [[125,543, 2,399], [2,997, 16,122]]. Route continuity of its classifier-only Top-1 path is 0.9335. Inference takes 2.4716 ms per GPS observation; the full refit took 0.2 s.

C8. Observation-level Top-1 accuracy, macro F1 and log loss (student-test)

Top-1 = 0.8663 (on reliably-labelled observations: 0.8675), macro F1 = 0.9178, log loss = 0.1102. CV (5-fold GroupKFold over the 80 training trips): macro F1 0.8926 ± 0.0433 (folds 0.8157-0.9184), Top-1 on labelled observations 0.8734.

C9. Overfitting or underfitting? Evidence

Training-fold F1 is 1.000 by construction (distance weighting), so it says nothing. The usable evidence is CV macro F1 0.8926 compared with student-test macro F1 0.9178, plus its higher log loss (0.1102). Its probabilities are less sharp, which is mild underfitting of the ranking task rather than overfitting.

3.2.3 Random Forest (Ensemble (bagging))

A1. Why did you consider this algorithm suitable for the PathSnap problem?

Tree ensembles capture interactions between features automatically, for example "small distance AND matching heading AND same arc close to the previous point". Bagging many deep trees reduces variance, which matters because trips from different cities differ a lot.

A2. Major strengths and limitations for this task

Strengths: strong accuracy with little tuning, robust to feature scaling and outliers, low variance across folds, fast batch inference. Limitations: largest model file, the slowest training among the strong models, and probabilities that are less sharp than boosting's (vote averaging).

B5. Important hyperparameters and initial values

n_estimators = 300, min_samples_leaf = 5, max_features = sqrt, max_depth = None, bootstrap = True, all training rows.

B6. Loss function / optimisation objective

Each tree greedily minimises Gini impurity; the forest averages the leaf class frequencies.

C7. Performance on the student-testing dataset

3.2.4 LightGBM (gradient boosting) (Boosting)

A1. Why did you consider this algorithm suitable for the PathSnap problem?

Gradient-boosted trees are usually the strongest learners on tabular, heterogeneous features like ours, and LightGBM's histogram algorithm handles about 650k training rows in seconds. Sequential error correction can focus on the hard cases: parallel roads, two-way streets and junctions.

A2. Major strengths and limitations for this task

Strengths: best student-test Top-1 and log loss, fast training and inference, well-calibrated probabilities for the HMM. Limitations: many interacting hyperparameters (hence the Bayesian search), it can overfit with many leaves, and it is less interpretable than the linear model (we report feature importances instead).

Binary log loss, minimised by gradient boosting with second-order (Newton) leaf values and L2 leaf regularisation.

5. HMM and Viterbi Route Reconstruction

1. Hidden states

For GPS observation t, the hidden states are its candidate arcs (up to 12). Each state carries the arc id and the projected position along the arc (proj_frac), so the candidate-to-road-segment correspondence is preserved, and the decoded state is the predicted road segment.

2. Emission probabilities

log et(s) = w * log p(s), where p(s) is the selected classifier's probability that candidate s is correct (clipped at 1e-6) and w is the emission weight.

3. Transition probabilities

log a(st-1, st) = -|dnet - dgps| / beta, following Newson and Krumm. dgps is the straight distance between the two GPS points. dnet is the distance along the directed network: on the same arc it is the forward progress; otherwise it is the remaining part of arc A, plus the Dijkstra shortest path from end(A) to start(B) (search cutoff 400 m), plus the travelled part of arc B. Wrong-way moves and unreachable arcs get probability 0. Viterbi recurrence: deltat(j) = maxi[deltat-1(i) + log a(i,j)] + log et(j), with back-pointers for the final backtrack.

4. Missing candidates and large GPS gaps

Every observation has at least one candidate (fallback to the nearest arcs). If dt > 10 s, the Dijkstra cutoff is widened to max(400 m, 3 * dgps + 200 m) and beta is multiplied by dt, so long gaps are not over-penalised.

5. Disconnected road segments

If no candidate pair between t-1 and t is reachable, the chain is closed at its best state and restarted from the emissions at t (counted as a "break"). This happens at holes in the network or GPS jumps. The decoder never fails and always returns one segment per observation.

Averaged over the 10 trips (mean of per-trip values, so it differs slightly from the pooled Top-1 in section 3.1), Top-1 goes from 0.9109 to 0.9234, route continuity from 0.9434 to 0.9714, and arc-set F1 (sequence level) from 0.8920 to 0.9163. A likely cause is that when the correct candidate is missing or weak for several consecutive points, the decoder keeps a connected but wrong branch for a while, whereas the per-point classifier recovers immediately. This is worth inspecting on the Folium map. Continuity stays below 1.0 because, at 1 Hz, the vehicle can traverse several short arcs between two observations; those arcs are connected through the network but are not consecutive in the per-observation list.

7. API, UI and Deployment

Laptop 1 runs uvicorn api.main:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8000. It loads artifacts/final_model.joblib and hmm_config.json and serves GET /trips (the trip folders in PATHSNAP_EVAL_DIR) and POST /predict {"trip_id"}. For each request the backend builds the candidates and features, runs the classifier, decodes with Viterbi, and only then scores the result against the reference route on the server. The response contains the GPS points, the predicted route polyline and the metrics. The reference route is never returned. Laptop 2 runs streamlit run ui/app.py with PATHSNAP_API_URL pointing to Laptop 1.

Development screenshots were taken on one machine with the API on 127.0.0.1. For the final submission, replace them with screenshots from the two-laptop setup (UI on Laptop 2, uvicorn console on Laptop 1).
